Hydraulic care guide · 5-minute read

Milky hydraulic oil? Here's what it means and how to fix it.

You pulled the dipstick or checked the sight glass and the oil looks like a coffee milkshake. That's your machine telling you something. Good news: it's common, it's fixable, and you can usually handle it yourself in an afternoon.

The short answer

Milky oil almost always means water got into your hydraulic system. Drain it, refill with fresh oil, treat it with Hydraulic Oil Fix, and drop a water absorber in the tank so it doesn't come back.

01The problem

Why your oil turned milky

Hydraulic oil can quietly hold a tiny bit of water without any sign of trouble. Past that point, the extra water can't dissolve anymore. It breaks into millions of little droplets that hang in the oil, and that's what turns it cloudy or milky.

So where does the water come from? Usually not from anything you did wrong.

Condensation

The big one. Your tank "breathes" air in and out as it heats up and cools down. Moisture in that air settles into the oil. Machines that sit for weeks, humid summers, and cold-to-warm winter swings make it worse.

Washing

A pressure washer aimed near the fill cap, breather, or worn seals can push water right in.

A loose or missing cap

Rain and dew find their way into any opening. A cracked cap or a missing breather is an open door.

Stored oil

An open jug or bucket of "fresh" oil sitting in a damp shed can pick up water before it ever reaches the machine.

Why it's worth fixing now

Oil's job is to keep a thin, slick film between metal parts that move against each other. Water thins that film out, so parts start rubbing harder than they should. It also leaves rust on the inside of your pump, valves, and cylinders. It clogs filters faster, and in freezing weather it can turn to ice inside the system.

Early on, you might notice the machine feels a little sluggish or the pump sounds louder than usual. Left alone, it shows up as worn-out parts and a repair bill.

½

A little water goes a long way. Industry testing shows that once water climbs to about 300 parts per million, the expected life of the bearings in a pump drops by about half. In a 20-gallon system, that's roughly a tablespoon and a half of water.Source: Pumps & Systems, based on bearing-life research. See sources below.

Quick check: water or air?

Air bubbles caught in the oil can also make it look cloudy, and that's a different fix. A two-minute test tells you which one you've got: pour a little oil into a clear glass jar and set it on a shelf.

Clears up within a day

That was air. Look for a low oil level, a leaky suction hose, or a loose fitting letting air in. This guide isn't the fix for that one.

Still cloudy the next day

That's water. Keep reading. The rest of this guide is for you.

02The fix

Five steps to clear oil that stays clear

You'll use two products that do two different jobs. Hydraulic Oil Fix deals with the water already in the oil. A Dry Hydraulics Water Absorber pulls out the water that keeps sneaking in. Together they fix the problem and keep it fixed.

  1. 1

    Find your total system capacity

    This is how much oil the whole system holds: tank, hoses, pump, and cylinders. Your owner's manual usually lists it. You'll need this number for the next steps, because the Oil Fix dose is based on the whole system, not just the tank.

  2. 2

    Drain and refill with fresh oil

    If the oil is milky, the Oil Fix label says to drain the system first and refill with fresh oil. Most owners swap the hydraulic filter while they're at it, since a filter that's been soaking in wet oil may already be partly clogged.

  3. 3

    Warm it up, then add Oil Fix at 3%

    Run the machine for a few minutes so the oil is warm and flowing. Add Hydraulic Oil Fix equal to 3% of your total system capacity, then run and work the hydraulics until the oil looks clear.

    Never let the Oil Fix in the system go over 3% of total capacity.

  4. 4

    Drop in a water absorber

    Slide the absorber into the tank and clip its tether to the fill cap so you can pull it out later. It sits there quietly soaking up new moisture before it can build back up.

  5. 5

    Keep it that way

    For routine protection, Oil Fix is dosed at 1% of system capacity. Swap the absorber about every 30 days with heavy use or high humidity, or about every 120 days if the machine is used now and then. If the milky look comes back fast, check the cap, breather, and seals for a leak.

04Common questions

Owners also ask

Can I just run the machine hard to cook the water out?

Getting the oil fully warm does drive off a little moisture, which is one reason machines that sit get this problem the most. But once the oil has turned milky, there's more water than heat can handle. The label's instruction is to drain, refill, then treat.

Is my machine ruined?

Usually not, especially if you caught it on a routine check. The damage from water builds up over time, which is why fixing it now matters. If the pump is noisy or the hydraulics are noticeably weak even after fresh oil, have a mechanic take a look.

Do I really need both products?

They do different jobs. Oil Fix takes care of the water that's already there. The absorber keeps new water from building back up. If your oil is clear today and you just want to keep it that way, the absorber plus a 1% routine dose of Oil Fix covers it.

Why does it keep coming back?

Condensation never fully stops, so some new water is normal. If the oil turns milky again within weeks, look for a direct leak: a cracked fill cap, a missing breather, worn seals, or pressure washing too close to the tank.
